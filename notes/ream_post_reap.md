# REAM (Router-weighted Expert Activation Merging) as a second stage after REAP-50

Scope note: REAM = arXiv 2604.04356 (v1, submitted 6 Apr 2026; Jha, Hashemzadeh, Saheb Pasand, Parviz, Lee, Knyazev; Mila / Samsung SAIL Montréal / Samsung AI Center Korea). The only version on arXiv is v1. An earlier (Feb 2026) blog version of the work by the corresponding author differs from the paper in calibration set, mixes and model list (it includes a Qwen3-235B result the paper drops) — both are cited below and the discrepancy is flagged. Largest scale per claim is stated inline.

## KQ1. The exact REAM algorithm (centroids, mix weights, "singleton" absorption, calibration statistics)

### Takeaway
REAM is a per-layer, retraining-free, one-shot method that keeps the N' highest-REAP-saliency experts as untouched centroids, greedily attaches up to C of the remaining (lowest-saliency) experts to the most similar centroid under a gate-logit + gated-output similarity, permutation-aligns them and saliency-weight-averages them into the centroid, deletes the absorbed experts' router rows, then re-runs the calibration forward through the merged layer before processing the next one ("sequential merging"). Because capacity (N'·C) far exceeds the number of non-centroids, most centroids receive nothing and remain singletons — "a few large groups and many singletons" — which is the structure the REAP authors themselves endorse.

### Cited Findings
- Saliency is REAP's score (Eq. 3): router weight × ‖E_i(x)‖₂ averaged over tokens where the expert is active; the N' highest-saliency experts per layer become centroids, sorted by decreasing saliency; "starting with c₁, each centroid greedily takes up to C unassigned non-centroid experts most similar to it" — [REAM paper, arXiv 2604.04356v1](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04356v1)
- Similarity (Eq. 7) δ_REAM(i,j) = δ_g(i,j) + δ̃_E(i,j): δ_g is the similarity of the two experts' gate-logit vectors over calibration tokens (Eq. 5); δ̃_E (Eq. 8) is the mean over tokens of sim(σ(x)_i·E_i(x), σ(x)_j·E_j(x)), i.e. similarity of *router-gated* expert outputs (cosine given as the example sim) — [REAM paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04356v1)
- Pseudo-pruning / singletons: "Because non-centroids are fewer than the total absorption capacity, most centroids get no assignments. These stay as singletons and pass through unchanged, yielding 'a few large groups and many singletons'" — [REAM paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04356v1)
- Settings: N' = 96 or 64 (from 128) for Qwen3-30B and GLM-4.5-Air; N' = 384 (from 512) for the 512-expert Qwen3-Next models; C = 32 for Qwen3-30B at 64 experts, C = 16 at 96 experts; C = 32 for Qwen3-Coder-Next and Qwen3-Next-80B; C = 16 for GLM-4.5-Air (Appendix A.1) — [REAM paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04356v1)
- Merge weights: Figure 1 caption calls absorption "saliency-weighted merging"; the only explicit weighting formula on the page is Eq. 6 (the baseline formula weighted by frequency saliency S^freq); the REAM section gives no separate formula, so the exact weighting is ambiguous in the paper text. Secondary summaries state "low-saliency experts contribute little to the weighted average, so the centroid dominates" — [REAM paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04356v1); [bknyaz blog](https://bknyaz.github.io//blog/2026/moe/)
- Weight alignment: Hungarian-style permutation matching with a combined cost C = C_act + C_wt, where C_act is the L2 distance between normalized activation vectors of neuron pairs and C_wt the L2 distance between weights; the permutation is applied to expert j before averaging — [REAM paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04356v1)
- Router: "Following REAP, gate rows for non-centroid experts are removed" (no summed/tied gates) — [REAM paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04356v1)
- Sequential merging: "After merging layer ℓ, a second forward pass recomputes activations for layer ℓ+1. Prior methods collect statistics once from the unmodified model" — [REAM paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04356v1)
- Calibration data (paper): 3072 sequences × 512 tokens from C4, NuminaMath and The-Stack-Smol in ten mixing ratios — [REAM paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04356v1). Calibration data (earlier blog version): 2048 sequences, C4 8% / NuminaMath-1.5 68% / The-Stack-smol 24% of tokens, with the proportions "may be suboptimal" — [bknyaz blog](https://bknyaz.github.io//blog/2026/moe/)
- Ablation (Fig. 4, 96 experts, mix 0.1:0.1:0.8): replacing REAP saliency with routing frequency: ΔAVG −8.7; removing gate-softmax scaling in the similarity: ΔAVG −5.9 / ΔGEN −11.5; removing pseudo-pruning: −3.6; removing δ_g: −1.4; removing sequential merging: −1.0; activation-only alignment: −0.5 — [REAM paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04356v1)
- Relationship to REAP's theory, from the REAP authors (Cerebras, mklasby, 2026-03-13): "REAM appears to be a valid approach and validates many of the arguments we made in our paper w.r.t. avoiding tied-router gates and encouraging singleton clusters" — [CerebrasResearch/reap issue #10](https://github.com/CerebrasResearch/reap/issues/10)
- MC-SMoE is "the method REAM reduces to when all its components are removed"; the REAM blog describes centroid selection as "similar to MC-SMoE" — [REAM paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04356v1); [bknyaz blog](https://bknyaz.github.io//blog/2026/moe/)

### Inferences
- Same forward pass as REAP? Partly. REAP's saliency needs only the top-k experts' outputs that the normal forward already computes. REAM additionally needs (a) per-token gate logits for *all* experts (free), (b) gated outputs σ_i·E_i(x) for expert *pairs* on common tokens for δ̃_E, and (c) per-neuron activation statistics for the alignment cost. Whether (b) is evaluated only on tokens where both experts are in the top-k or by densely running every expert on every calibration token is not stated in the paper text I could retrieve — this determines whether the statistics pass costs 1× or ~(N/k)× a normal forward. Mark as unresolved (see Gaps).
- The "sequential" pass doubles the forward cost relative to REAP (two propagations per MoE layer) but is only worth ΔAVG +1.0 in the paper's own ablation (vs +3.9 GEN on Qwen3-Coder-Next in Table 4: 72.9 vs 69.0). It is the component to drop first under a compute budget.
- Because merged weights are dominated by the high-saliency centroid, REAM at low merge fractions behaves like "REAP plus a small saliency-weighted correction", which is why it tracks REAP closely and flips sign at 50%.

### Gaps
- The exact merge-weight formula for REAM (vs the Eq. 6 baseline) is ambiguous in the retrievable paper text; the code (`merge.py`, `config.py`) would resolve it but was not read.
- Whether δ̃_E requires dense (all-expert) evaluation on calibration tokens is not stated.

## KQ2. Full results: models, ratios, benchmarks, vs REAP / HC-SMoE / M-SMoE, and the calibration-mix ablation (33.8 → 60.7)

### Takeaway
At 25% reduction (bf16, no fine-tune) REAM beats REAP on the generative average by +1.2 (Qwen3-30B), +1.9 (Qwen3-Next-80B), +2.2 (Qwen3-Coder-Next) and +2.0 (GLM-4.5-Air, 106B — the largest model in the paper). At 50% on Qwen3-30B, REAP wins GEN (best-over-mixes 62.8 vs 60.7; at the same best mix 62.8 vs 58.7) while REAM wins MC. ### Inferences
- Why REAM beats REAP by 0.8–2 GEN at 25%: the absorbed experts are the lowest-saliency 25%, whose saliency-weighted contribution barely perturbs the centroid; REAM therefore recovers a small amount of the signal REAP discards at near-zero risk. The ablation confirms the gain hinges on REAP saliency for centroid choice (−8.7 without it) and on gate-confidence scaling in the similarity (−11.5 GEN without it).
- Why it loses at 50%: at 64/128 the absorbed set now includes mid-saliency experts with distinct functions; averaging them into a centroid perturbs the centroid's function (the REAP paper's G_ij = E‖f_i − f_j‖² term grows), and C was doubled to 32 to allow it. REAP's loss at 50% is purely "missing experts"; REAM's is "missing experts + corrupted survivors". The paper does not explain this itself; the blog concedes merging is "still quite suboptimal".
- The stability "red flag" is not REAM-specific. At 64 experts, every mix with C4 ≥ 0.1 (except 0.1:0.1:0.8) collapses HumanEval/LCB to ≤ 16 for BOTH REAP and REAM; every C4-free mix keeps them ≥ 60. REAM's spread (33.8–60.7, Δ26.9) and REAP's (34.7–62.8, Δ28.1) are the same phenomenon. The domain-stratified, no-general-web corpora already used in this project (REAP corpus audit memo: general share 4.76%) are the right control; the paper's data say C4 fraction is the single variable to hold near zero for GEN.
- The 235B result being dropped between the blog and the paper (where REAP won) is a selection concern; the paper never states REAM wins above 106B.

### Gaps
- No REAM result at 50% on any model above 30B in the paper; no REAM result at any ratio above 235B (blog) / 106B (paper).
- MC-SMoE/M-SMoE are not benchmarked in the REAM paper; the only M-SMoE numbers are the REAP paper's.
- Figure 7 (Pareto at 64 experts) has no numeric hypervolume in the text.

## KQ4. Composition: REAM after REAP-50 (prune → heal → merge further)

### Takeaway
Nobody has published merge-after-prune. The REAP paper's error model says merging cost ∝ E[(g_i+g_j)²]·Var[r_ij]·E‖f_i−f_j‖²; after REAP-50 all three factors are pushed up for the survivors (renormalized gates are larger, the low-saliency "free to absorb" experts are already gone, and survivors are by construction the most distinct/high-magnitude ones), and healing fine-tune further specialises them. REAM's own measured trajectory (wins at 25%, loses at 50%, −13 points at 48% on a big FP4 model) already tracks that prediction before any pruning; a REAM stage on top of REAP-50 is therefore expected to behave like REAM at ≥ 55–62% cumulative reduction, which no one has shown to work. The only honest status is "untested, with theory and all extrapolatable evidence pointing the wrong way"; if tried, it should be a small (≤ 10%) absorb fraction with C small, measured on GEN/code, not MC.

### Cited Findings
- REAP theory: merged expert f̃ = α·f_i + (1−α)·f_j receives summed gate g_i+g_j; mixing ratio r(x) = g_i/(g_i+g_j); optimal α* = E[r]; error = E_x[(g_i+g_j)²·(r−α*)²·‖Δ_ij‖²] ≈ E[(g_i+g_j)²]·Var[r]·G_ij under weak-correlation assumptions; "every constant-α merge incurs positive error" whenever Var[r] > 0 and ‖Δ_ij‖ > 0; the policy-variability term only arises on inputs where both experts are selected; pruning "does not penalize policy variability because the router still controls surviving experts independently"; highly granular SMoEs "should be especially disadvantaged by merging" — [REAP paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.13999)
- REAP Appendix A hierarchical-clustering bound (Eq. 11): "The error grows with both the cluster's total gate-value and the variance of the dynamic mixture" — [REAP paper, appendix](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.13999)
- REAM does NOT use summed/tied gates — absorbed experts' router rows are deleted (REAP-style), so routing to the centroid is unchanged and the absorbed experts' tokens are re-routed by the renormalized top-k — [REAM paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04356v1); the REAP authors say this choice is why REAM "appears to be a valid approach" — [reap issue #10](https://github.com/CerebrasResearch/reap/issues/10)
- No prune-then-merge pipeline exists in any of the three codebases: the Samsung repo has only `merge.py` with `--merge_size`; puwaer exposes `merging="none"` (= REAP) or REAM as alternatives, "not a combined workflow"; Akicou exposes `--method {prune,merge}` separately — [Samsung README](https://github.com/SamsungSAILMontreal/ream); [puwaer README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/puwaer/moe-expert-compress/main/README.md); [Akicou README](https://github.com/Akicou/ream)
- Cerebras has not integrated REAM: the June 2026 request "Would you be interested in looking into integrating it into REAP?" on issue #10 is unanswered and the issue is open — [reap issue #10](https://github.com/CerebrasResearch/reap/issues/10)
- REAP paper: cluster-size caps do not rescue merging — "MC accuracy remains stable up to a maximum cluster size of 4, generative coding capabilities are severely diminished by restricting the clustering algorithm" (Fig. A7) — [REAP paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.13999)
- The REAP authors' largest-scale results use pruning only: Kimi-K2-Instruct-W4A16 (1026.4B) code avg 0.640 @25% / 0.624 @50% vs base 0.631; MC 0.773 / 0.643 vs 0.780; Qwen3-Coder-480B code 0.624 / 0.619 vs 0.636 — no merging method was run at that scale — [REAP paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.13999)

### Inferences (explicitly extrapolation)
- Three mechanisms make post-REAP-50 merging worse than fresh merging at the same absolute expert count:
  1. Gate magnitude: after REAP removes half the router rows, top-k softmax renormalises over survivors, so E[(g_i+g_j)²] for any surviving pair rises (the removed experts' probability mass is redistributed). The REAP error term scales with this quadratically.
  2. Saliency truncation: REAM's gain comes from absorbing experts with tiny saliency whose weighted contribution ≈ 0. After REAP-50 the saliency distribution's lower half is gone; the next-lowest 10–25% of survivors are mid-saliency experts, so each absorption perturbs the centroid materially (this is exactly the 25% → 50% transition in the paper, where REAM flipped from +1.2 to −4.1 GEN vs REAP at the best mix).
  3. Healing fine-tune: any post-REAP SFT/distillation re-specialises survivors on the pruned routing, increasing pairwise Δ_ij and Var[r] relative to the un-healed model; calibration statistics for REAM must then be re-collected on the healed model (another full calibration pass), and the healed router is the one that must be re-truncated.
- Counter-argument worth stating: the Var[r] term only exists on tokens where both experts are in the top-k. If post-REAP-50 routing is very peaked (top-k mass concentrated), co-activation support shrinks and the "static gap" term dominates — which REAM's gated similarity (δ̃_E with σ scaling, worth −11.5 GEN when removed) is designed to pick for. This is the only lever that could make a small post-REAP absorb cheap; it predicts absorbing only pairs with near-disjoint routing support and near-identical outputs — which in a 50%-pruned, healed model may simply not exist in useful numbers.
- Expert arithmetic for the target class (illustrative, Kimi-K2-like 384 experts/layer): REAP-50 → 192; REAM absorbing 10% of survivors → 173 (55% cumulative); 25% → 144 (62.5% cumulative). The paper's only 50% point (64/128) already lost on GEN; 62.5% cumulative is outside every measured REAM regime.
- If a composition experiment is run anyway: keep sequential merging ON (it is what distinguishes REAM from the failed HC/M-SMoE merges in the REAP paper), set C ≤ 8, absorb ≤ 10%, calibrate with zero general-web text, and gate acceptance on HumanEval/LCB-style code and a reasoning set, since MC moves the wrong way and hides GEN collapse in every table above.

### Gaps
- No empirical data point for merge-after-prune on any model; no theoretical treatment of merging on a renormalised, post-pruning router.
- Whether healing fine-tune increases or decreases pairwise expert similarity is unmeasured (the project's own quant-drift memo shows hidden-state cos ~0.9 between quant variants, suggesting representational drift is large).

## KQ5. Pre-quantized (FP4 / MXFP4 / FP8) experts: block-scale reconciliation and dequant→merge→repack cost

### Takeaway
REAP's authors state outright that merging "necessitates re-quantization for block quantization formats that share common scaling coefficients" while pruning is bit-exact; the REAM paper is silent on quantization (it only says quantization "can be applied after"). The one implementation that handles FP4 experts (puwaer, DeepSeek-V4 e2m1 + UE8M0 per-32 scales) dequantises each expert, merges, and re-snaps to the FP4 grid, and reports that the snap error "is comparable to the original quantization error" — i.e. merged experts pay a second quantization pass. On Thor the whole operation must be layer-streamed; the raw byte traffic is small (tens of minutes) but the dense calibration forward is the real cost (KQ6).

### Cited Findings
- REAP Appendix E: "combining expert merging with quantization is more complex; the fine-grained shared quantization scales across parameter groups must be reconciled during merging, whereas pruning requires no such adjustment"; "Pruning is easily combined with quantization, as demonstrated by our large-scale evaluations"; Kimi-K2 results "combine 4-bit weights with 50% expert pruning, representing a total size reduction of 87.5%" — [REAP paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.13999)
- REAM paper: quantization not covered; "quantization and SVD can be applied after pruning or merging and are complementary to REAM" — [REAM paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04356v1)
- puwaer FP4 handling: "Routed experts are FP4 (e2m1 packed two per byte, one UE8M0 scale per [1, 32] row group)", other weights FP8 e4m3 on [128,128] blocks; "experts are dequantized one at a time"; "REAP is bit-exact — dropping an expert slices both the weight and its scale grid"; for REAM "a merged expert is snapped back onto the FP4 grid" and with only eight e2m1 magnitudes "that snap is comparable to the original quantization error", which "can offset some of the gain merging provides over pruning"; singletons are untouched so only merged experts pay it; without the `kernels` package a dequantising fallback forward is used that "skips activation quantization" — [puwaer README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/puwaer/moe-expert-compress/main/README.md)
- puwaer `--streaming`: loads one decoder layer at a time, "peak memory is the skeleton plus one layer"; "DeepSeek-V4-Flash is 155 GiB; streaming compresses it inside a 100 GiB job"; bit-identical to the resident path; "The cost is roughly one extra pass over the weights"; requires the adapter to declare `uniform_layer_inputs` (only DeepSeek-V4 does); `device_map="auto"` offload "silently corrupts results" (meta tensors) and is rejected; no distributed compression — [puwaer README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/puwaer/moe-expert-compress/main/README.md)
- The Hungarian alignment permutes neurons of expert j (rows of up/gate, columns of down) before averaging — [REAM paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04356v1)

### Inferences (extrapolation, arithmetic shown)
- Why block scales are the problem: a per-32-row-group UE8M0 (power-of-two) scale is shared by 32 weights. Averaging two experts' weights requires (i) dequantising both to bf16 (exact), (ii) permuting expert j's neurons — which moves weights across scale groups along the permuted dimension, so the original scale grid of j no longer applies, (iii) averaging, (iv) choosing a fresh scale per 32-group and re-snapping to e2m1. Step (iv) is a full second quantisation of the merged expert; the centroid's own weights also get re-snapped because the sum shifts them off their grid. With NVFP4 (FP8 e4m3 scales per 16 + a per-tensor fp32 scale) the same applies with a 16-element group; with MXFP4 (UE8M0 per 32) it is the DeepSeek-V4 case exactly. Pre-FP4 experts (the V4.1-Flash / MiMo-V2.6 class rejected in this project's shortlist memo) cannot be merged "for free" the way they can be pruned.
- Error accounting: the project's own measurement that NVFP4 costs ~+1.56% ppl on GLM-5.3 dense weights (NVFP4 overlay memo) is the order of a second snap on merged experts; puwaer's claim that the snap "is comparable to the original quantization error" implies merged experts carry ~2× the quantisation noise of singletons. At a 10% absorb fraction that touches ~10% of expert weight; at 25% ~25%.
- Byte cost on a 1T model at ~0.62 B/w (project's measured NVFP4 density): ~620 GB of expert weight. Dequantise-merge-repack per layer is a streaming read + write ≈ 2 × 620 GB; at 3 GB/s NVMe ≈ 7 min per pass, so ~15 min total — negligible. Hungarian solves: with d_ff = 2048 (Kimi-K2) the LAPJV cost is O(d_ff³) ≈ 8.6 GFLOP each; absorbing 25% of 384 experts over 61 layers = 5,856 solves ≈ 50 TFLOP on CPU, i.e. tens of minutes to a few hours single-threaded, parallelisable. Neither is the long pole; the calibration forward is (KQ6).
- Thor-specific: puwaer's streaming mode requires `uniform_layer_inputs` and is implemented only for DeepSeek-V4; for GLM-5.x/Kimi it would have to be written, and the project's unified-memory OOM memo (never co-run beside a vLLM server; driver pool only reclaimable by drop_caches) means the 122 GiB budget must hold skeleton + one bf16-dequantised layer (Kimi-K2 layer: 384×3×7168×2048 ≈ 16.9B params ≈ 33.8 GB bf16) + the calibration hidden-state cache (KQ6: ~22.5 GB) + attention working set. That fits, but not with a server resident.

### Gaps
- No author of any REAM implementation has quantified the FP4 re-snap penalty in isolation (ablating merge-in-bf16-then-quantise vs merge-on-prequantised).
- NVFP4/MXFP4 "scale reconciliation" strategies smarter than re-snap (e.g. choosing the merged scale to minimise error given both parents) are not discussed anywhere found.

## KQ6. Compute: scaling REAM's ~1.5 h / ~30 GB on 30B to a 1T model

### Takeaway
The paper's only cost figure is Qwen3-30B-A3B: ~1 h non-sequential, ~1.5 h sequential, ~30 GB VRAM (GPU type unstated), on 3072×512 ≈ 1.57M calibration tokens. Scaling by active parameters (3B → 32B, ×10.7), expert pairs per layer (128² → 384², ×9 for the similarity term) and layers (48 → 61) gives roughly 15–25 GPU-hours on H100-class hardware for a 1T model, i.e. of order 2–5 days on a single Thor if the dense similarity pass is required — extrapolation, no measured point exists above 30B except "6×H100/H200, runtime not stated" for the 129B and 300B community runs.

### Cited Findings
- "Qwen3-30B-A3B-Instruct-2507: non-sequential merging takes about 1 hour; sequential takes about 1.5 hours. Both use about 30 GB VRAM"; no timing for 80B/106B; calibration 3072 sequences × 512 tokens — [REAM paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.04356v1)
- Sequential merging "requires propagating calibration data through each MoE layer twice"; evaluating the 235B model required 8×H100; 30B needed 1–4 GPUs per task, "from a few minutes to 1-2 hours" per task — [bknyaz blog](https://bknyaz.github.io//blog/2026/moe/)
- Community: Qwen3.8-Flash-Next (129B output) on 6×H100 SXM 80GB, runtime not reported; Hy3 300B → 104B on 6×H200 141GB with 3 TB RAM, runtime not reported, with `--cpu-merge` (merge on CPU) — [HF card 1](https://huggingface.co/Akicou/Qwen3.8-Flash-Next-REAM-60Pct); [HF card 2](https://huggingface.co/Akicou/Hy3-REAM-100B)
- puwaer: 155 GiB DeepSeek-V4-Flash compressed inside a 100 GiB job via streaming; "Compression run times: Not reported"; base-model eval needed 4 GH200 nodes, compressed 2 — [puwaer README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/puwaer/moe-expert-compress/main/README.md)
- REAP itself (Cerebras) calibrates with far more data: 12,228 samples (non-reasoning) or 24,456 (reasoning, +Open-R1 Mixture-of-Thoughts) at up to 16,384 tokens — [REAP paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.13999)

### Inferences (extrapolation, arithmetic shown)
- Forward cost: tokens T = 1.57M (paper) → 2 passes (sequential) → active params 3B (30B-A3B) vs ~32B (Kimi-K2-class) → 2·T·2·A FLOPs ≈ 2 × 1.57e6 × 2 × 32e9 ≈ 2.0e17 FLOP ≈ 200 PFLOP; at an effective 200 TFLOP/s (H100 bf16 at ~20% MFU on batch-512 prefill) ≈ 17 min — the forward is NOT the long pole if only top-k experts are evaluated. If δ̃_E needs dense all-expert outputs, multiply by N/k = 384/8 = 48 → ~13 h on H100-class, and the 30 GB / 1.5 h figure on 30B (128/8 = 16×) is consistent with a dense or near-dense pass, which is why the paper's run is 1.5 h and not minutes. Mark: unverified which it is.
- Thor: the project's measured GLM-5.3 decode (~12 tok/s single-stream) is irrelevant; prefill-style batched throughput on Thor for a 1T NVFP4 model has not been measured in this project. Taking Thor at ~1/5 of an H100 for bf16 prefill (dense 2,070 TFLOPS marketing is FP4 sparse; bf16 dense is far lower) gives ~1.5 h for a top-k-only pass and ~2.5–3 days for a dense pass, per REAM run. Each calibration-mix variant is a separate run, so a 3–5-mix sweep (needed, per KQ2, to control the stability problem) is 1–2 weeks of Thor time for the dense case. Treat as order-of-magnitude.
- Memory: hidden-state cache for sequential merging = T × d_model × 2 B = 1.57e6 × 7168 × 2 ≈ 22.5 GB (Kimi-K2 d=7168) per layer boundary, plus per-expert activation statistics for the alignment cost (d_ff × N per layer, small), plus one dequantised bf16 layer (~34 GB for 384 experts × 3 × 7168×2048) → ~60–70 GB working set, which fits the 122 GiB envelope only with nothing else resident (see unified-memory OOM memo). Using the project's larger REAP calibration corpus instead of 1.57M tokens scales the cache linearly — 24k samples × 16k tokens would be 250× larger and must be chunked.
- Hungarian/alignment and byte traffic: ≤ a few hours CPU and ≤ ~15 min NVMe respectively (KQ5), not the bottleneck.

### Gaps
- GPU type behind the paper's 1.5 h figure is unstated; no runtime above 30B is reported by anyone.
- Whether the Samsung `merge.py` evaluates every expert on every calibration token (dense) was not verified from code.

## Bottom line for the 1T-on-Thor plan (synthesis across KQs)

- REAM's demonstrated win is narrow: +0.8–2.2 GEN at 25% expert reduction, bf16, ≤ 106B (author-run). Every independent or larger-scale comparison found (235B blog, DeepSeek-V4-Flash 280B FP4 at 30%/48%, DeepSeek-VL2 at ρ ≤ 0.625) has REAP ≥ REAM; at ~50% REAM is 4 points (30B, bf16) to 13 points (280B, FP4) behind REAP on generative tasks.
- The 33.8 → 60.7 swing is not a REAM pathology: REAP swings 34.7 → 62.8 on the same table. It is the C4 share of calibration (r ≈ −0.85 with GEN), which the project's domain-stratified, ~5%-general corpora already control; code-heavy, C4-free mixes (0:0.3:0.7, 0:0.5:0.5) were the stable, high-GEN ones for both methods.
- REAM on top of REAP-50 has no precedent; REAP's error model (gate² × Var[r] × expert gap) predicts it to be worse than fresh REAM at the same count, and fresh REAM already loses at 50%. Pre-FP4 experts add a second quantisation on every merged expert (REAP authors' Appendix E; puwaer's measured "comparable to original quantization error"). Cost is dominated by the calibration forward (extrapolated ~hours top-k-only to ~days dense per mix on Thor), not by bytes or Hungarian solves.
- If a composition experiment is still wanted: absorb ≤ 10% of survivors, C ≤ 8, sequential ON, zero C4, merge in bf16 then quantise once, gate on code/reasoning GEN not MC, and compare against simply pruning the same 10% with REAP (which, in every ≥ 50% datapoint above, is the stronger operator).
