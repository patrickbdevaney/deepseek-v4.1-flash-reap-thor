# Shrink the expert union, not the drafter

The server has no cheap kernel headroom left. At 25 tok/s × 8.57 GB/token it already moves about 214 GB/s, which is roughly 94% of the 228 GB/s the project measures and 78% of Thor's 273 GB/s datasheet figure. Every remaining step change therefore has to **reduce bytes per committed token**. Three levers can do that. First, read fewer routed experts per token: training-free top-6→top-3 with corrected renormalization, worth about **1.15–1.25x AR** on this model's byte ledger, though evidence is MMLU-only so far. Second, read fewer *unique* experts per verify block: a verifier expert budget or a lower k, which is what decides whether spec decode wins on chat. Third, avoid re-reading the prompt at all: a session radix prefix cache, an order-of-magnitude TTFT lever on agentic traffic where published hit rates exceed 90%. The owner's per-request adaptive speculative decoding is correct in spirit, but as specified it is a *floor*, not a multiplier. Switching spec off per request only lifts chat from 0.98x to 1.00x. The version worth building decides **per verify round**, in the Cascade style (spec off when utility < 1, K chosen otherwise). That version has measured evidence of +7–14% over static K, caps worst-case slowdown at about 5%, and becomes more valuable as the other levers cut verify cost. The literature contains **no lossless ≥2x chat speedup for a fine-grained MoE at batch 1 with all experts resident**. The realistic stacked target is about 30 tok/s AR and 1.3x (lossless) to about 1.7–2x (lossy verifier budget) on mixed chat. Code, math and agentic turns go higher, and vision and long-context work are mostly build-out rather than speed.

## Bandwidth is spent, so only byte cuts move decode

The current byte ledger rules out most of the kernel-efficiency literature. The best published batch-1 persistent megakernels reach about **78–80% of memory bandwidth end to end**, against at most 50–69% for CUDA-graph stacks ([Hazy Research](https://hazyresearch.stanford.edu/blog/2025-05-27-no-bubbles); [MPK, OSDI '26](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.22219v1)). This engine already sits at or above that level. Its own logs show the two-stage greedy head (NVFP4 shortlist plus bit-identical bf16 rescore) already harvested, taking AR from 22.24 to 24.52 tok/s. Glue and the KV branch already run overlapped on side streams ([internal log](/home/patrickd/.claude/projects/-home-patrickd/memory/v41-cuda-zenith-mandate.md)). Two of the top levers in the decode-kernel research ledger are therefore already done: the "~4 ms LM-head shortlist" and the "3–5 ms glue fusion". The research ledger pre-dates them. Pure bandwidth tricks are capped by arithmetic: 100% of 228 GB/s is 26.6 tok/s, and 100% of the 273 GB/s spec is 31.9 tok/s, a ceiling of **1.06–1.28x** ([Thor spec, RidgeRun](https://developer.ridgerun.com/wiki/index.php/NVIDIA_Jetson_Thor)). L2 persistence has a documented negative result: a llama.cpp PR slowed down because streamed weights evicted the persisted set ([torad-labs PR #82](https://github.com/torad-labs/llama.cpp/pull/82)). A STREAM-style read probe on Thor is still worth an afternoon, because it fixes the denominator every other estimate uses. NVIDIA's own TensorRT-Edge-LLM batch-1 numbers on Thor imply only about 70% of peak, so the engine already beats the vendor stack ([TRT-Edge-LLM benchmarks](https://nvidia.github.io/TensorRT-Edge-LLM/0.11.0/user_guide/performance/performance-benchmarks.html)).

The strongest AR lever found is a router change, not a kernel. "Training-Free Halving of Activated Experts" activates k₁ experts but normalizes by the probability mass of k₂ > k₁. Halving Qwen3.5-397B-A17B from 10 to 5 experts costs **0.55 MMLU points**. Qwen3.6-35B-A3B at 8→4 loses 0.35 points with the fix, against 4.65 with naive renormalization ([arXiv 2609.04575](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.04575)). A NeurIPS 2026 controlled study finds that no dynamic expert-skipping method consistently beats a static top-k cut at matched expert count. It also finds that GSM8K and HumanEval collapse below an effective k of about 2, and that even 8→1 experts bought only **1.22x** wall-clock at batch 1 because non-expert bytes stay put ([arXiv 2610.28369](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.28369v1)). On this model routed experts are about 3.0 GB of the 8.57 GB/token, so f ≈ 0.35. Top-6→top-3 then saves about 1/(1−0.175) ≈ **1.2x** (an estimate from the ledger). Static top-5 in deeper layers buys only about 2–5%. Two risks are specific to this model. The router is noaux_tc, so the k₂ renormalization must apply to the unbiased sigmoid weights, not the biased selection scores ([internal note](/home/patrickd/.claude/projects/-home-patrickd/memory/noaux-tc-router-formula.md)). And REAP-192 has already removed half the experts, so the survivors may carry less slack than unpruned Qwen. Gate it on generative and reasoning evals with a pinned generation config, never on MMLU.

The remaining byte cuts are real but small. Trellis codes (QTIP, the basis of EXL3 and ik_llama's IQ*_KT) lead quality per bit at 2–3 bpw. QTIP-3 beats AQLM-3 and QuIP#-3 on every Llama-2 size, and QTIP-2 approaches their 3-bit quality ([QTIP](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.11235v4)). The realistic expert gain is "same quality at about 0.5 bpw less": roughly 0.5 GB or 2 ms per token. Decode costs 2–4 ALU instructions per weight, and the only low-compute evidence is a warning: llama.cpp K-quants beat EXL3 by 23–36% on an RTX 3060 ([atomic.chat](https://atomic.chat/blog/guides/exl3-vs-gguf)). A 20-SM micro-benchmark must come before any conversion. Lossless entropy coding helps only on bf16/fp8 tensors (ZipServ: 1.31x kernel and 1.22x end to end on Ada, [ZipServ](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2603.17435v1)). On already-low-bit experts the headroom is probably 5–15%, with no batch-1 measurement anywhere ([arXiv 2606.15789](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.15789)). TEAL-style activation sparsity cannot skip 256-wide K-quant super-blocks, so it does not transfer to the 2-bit experts ([TEAL](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.14690v2)).

## Verify pays for the union of experts, which sets spec decode's ceiling

Speculative decoding on this model is priced by the **union of routed experts across the k+1 verified tokens**, not by drafter quality. Cohere's production MoE (top-8 of 128) touches about 20.4 unique experts for 4 verify tokens, 2.55x a single token rather than 4x, with adjacent-token overlap of 0.38 ([Cohere](https://cohere.com/blog/mixture-of-experts-models-get-more-from-speculative-decoding)). A 10-model study puts 4-token unions at **2.4x top-k (Qwen3-30B) up to 2.9x (Kimi-K2.5, 384 experts)**. Temporal overlap cuts the union 21–34% below independence, and routing signals cannot predict rejection, because accepted and rejected overlap distributions coincide about 85% ([Ranjan et al., arXiv 2610.38981](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.38981)). DeepSeek routing is the worst case measured. On DeepSeek-V3.1 with MTP at γ=4, the union is 31.4 experts per layer for top-8, about 3.9x k. Cost-aware lossless tree selection (EcoSpec) moved it only to 31.2 and the speedup from **1.10x to 1.15x** ([EcoSpec](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.12696)).

The engine's own profile matches this. A K=3 round costs about 90 ms: 12.4 ms of drafting and 77 ms of verify, against roughly 40 ms per AR step. The verify experts already run expert-major at bandwidth over the union bytes, so the "L2 reuse across a verify block" lever is already in the kernel ([internal log](/home/patrickd/.claude/projects/-home-patrickd/memory/v41-cuda-zenith-mandate.md)). Spec decode breaks even only when a round commits more than about **2.25 tokens**. Measured K=3 speedups by domain are 1.60x on math, 1.43x on code, 1.21x on agentic and 0.98x on chat. These imply about 3.6 committed tokens per round on math and about 2.2 on chat, which sits on the break-even line. This is the same wall the GLM-5.3 server hit, where spec was bounded at 1.27x even at perfect acceptance ([internal note](/home/patrickd/.claude/projects/-home-patrickd/memory/glm53-spec-decode-moe-wall.md)).

The drafter is therefore not the gap. Block drafters lead the 2026 field. DFlash reaches τ 6.49 and 4.86x on dense Qwen3-8B (H200, batch 1), against EAGLE-3's τ ≈ 3 ([DFlash](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.06036)). On MoE the lead shrinks: 2.6–3.5x on Qwen3-Coder-30B-A3B at concurrency 1, and only 1.3–1.7x on GPT-OSS-120B at c=8. The DFlash README itself advises **block ≤ 5 for quantized targets** because verify width hurts quantized matmul ([z-lab/dflash](https://github.com/z-lab/dflash)). Every bandwidth-bound MoE batch-1 study lands at 1.1–1.4x over EAGLE-3/MTP ([EVICT](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.00342v1); [EcoSpec](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.12696)). DeepSeek's native DSpark is already a block drafter with a per-position confidence head ([lilting.ch on DSpark](https://lilting.ch/en/articles/deepseek-v4-pro-dspark-speculative-decoding)). Swapping in DFlash or EAGLE-3.1 changes nothing structural.

Draft-head fine-tuning should drop down the graph. The literature recipe is sound: regenerate data with the *modified* target and train against its frozen hidden states. EDA recovers τ 4.37→4.79 at 61% of full-retrain cost ([EDA](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.09527v1)). "Quantize the Target, Quantize the Drafter" stages drafter training on the frozen quantized target ([arXiv 2607.04244](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2607.04244)). This project already ran that experiment, and it came back negative. Six fine-tune arms, including the 0731 recipe, never beat the native head's validation loss on 114K anchors of served-tap greedy traces. A separate NVFP4-drafter arm lost acceptance in every domain ([internal log](/home/patrickd/.claude/projects/-home-patrickd/memory/v41-cuda-zenith-mandate.md)). Even a successful fine-tune only raises τ, and τ pays only when verify cost per committed token falls. The one loss worth a further arm is DraftExpert-style **router-agreement distillation**, which trains the drafter to predict the target's experts. DraftExpert reaches 84–87% acceptance and 86–88% verifier-expert prediction hit rates, but only on 16B models under offload ([DraftExpert](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.24434)). It would feed a verifier budget rather than τ.

## Adaptive spec decode works as a per-round controller, not a per-request switch

The owner's proposal is to decide at inference time whether a completion runs with spec decode. It rests on the right observation: spec decode pays on some content and loses on other content. The Cascade paper (NVIDIA/Georgia Tech) gives the strongest evidence. Draft tokens raise MoE verify time 2–3x, and vanilla spec decode slows decoding by **up to 1.5x**. A utility controller that disables speculation when token gain divided by verify cost falls below 1, and otherwise picks K, **limits slowdown to 5% and gains 7–14% over static K** across five MoEs in vLLM ([arXiv 2506.20675](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.20675)). The same paper finds that expert-token ratio and speculation cost show short-horizon temporal locality, which is what makes an online estimate trustworthy.

Run against the measured domain split, a per-request switch is nearly worthless. With perfect foresight it turns chat's 0.98x into 1.00x, and it leaves math (1.60x), code (1.43x) and agentic (1.21x) where they already are. In this regime a per-request switch saves about 2% on chat and nothing elsewhere. Worse, the decision would rest on a prompt classifier or an initial acceptance probe, and acceptance varies *within* a completion more than between completions. A chat answer contains formulaic spans, code blocks, quoted context and boilerplate where acceptance is high, mixed with open-ended prose where it is not. The DSpark confidence head already estimates per-position acceptance, which SpecDec++ showed works as a stopping rule (2.04–2.26x vs 1.90x on Llama-2-chat, [SpecDec++](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.19715v3)). The controller worth building therefore acts **per verify round**. It keeps an EMA of committed tokens per round and reads the confidence head over the current draft. It chooses K ∈ {0,1,2,3} to maximize expected committed tokens divided by a round-cost table indexed by K, measured on Thor (today about 1.0, ~1.4–1.5, ~2.25 AR-equivalents for K=0, 1, 3). A per-request prior, such as the domain or whether a tool schema is present, only seeds the estimate. The engine's measured profile shows **K=1 is roughly as good as K=3** for this union structure. The research cost model gives (1+a)/1.42 ≈ 1.23x at a = 0.75, so the controller should fall back to K=1 often rather than jump straight to K=0. Expect chat to move from 0.98x to about 1.05–1.1x and mixed workloads to gain the Cascade-like 7–14% over static K. Both are extrapolations from Cascade and the cost model, not Thor measurements.

Two engineering details decide whether it works. First, when spec is off, the drafter still needs its context. DSpark injects target-layer features (taps 37/38/39) into its own state. AR steps compute those layers anyway, so the controller should **store taps for skipped positions and batch-fill the drafter on re-entry**, rather than run the 12.4 ms draft every step or re-prefill the drafter from scratch. Second, the controller must keep a small exploration budget. A periodic K=1 probe while spec is off stops it latching off after a low-acceptance stretch. Rollback hygiene carries over unchanged. SGLang doubles compressor ring sizes under spec so draft writes cannot clobber live slots ([LMSYS DeepSeek-V4](https://www.lmsys.org/blog/2026-04-25-deepseek-v4/)), and any cache snapshot must be taken only at accepted positions.

The controller composes with every other spec lever because each one lowers the round-cost table it optimizes over. Top-k halving roughly halves the union bytes in verify. A verifier expert budget caps the union multiplier. AcceptMoE weights router demand by offline commitment probability and self-sizes the expert set, giving **1.29x over EAGLE-3 with all experts resident** at batch 1 for a mean −0.27 pp accuracy ([AcceptMoE](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.02989)). MoE-Spec's top-B budget gives +10–30% ([MoE-Spec](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.16052v1)). SuffixDecoding removes the draft term entirely: its drafts come from a CPU suffix tree, giving up to **3.9x on agentic benchmarks** and about 2.2x over EAGLE-2/3, with hybrids that switch drafter by match confidence ([SuffixDecoding, NeurIPS 2025](https://neurips.cc/virtual/2025/poster/115453)). With zero draft cost, break-even falls from about 2.25 to about 1.93 committed tokens per round, and long matched suffixes clear it easily. As these levers drop the round cost, more of each completion clears break-even. The controller's job then shifts from preventing losses to picking K and choosing the drafter (DSpark vs suffix) per round. Lossy typical acceptance is a further measured +11% on linear DFlash at T=0.3, with no matched-quality eval yet ([internal note](/home/patrickd/.claude/projects/-home-patrickd/memory/dflash-typical-acceptance-win.md)). Stacked, the research projects about **1.6–2.2x on mixed chat only with the lossy verifier budget**, and about 1.3x lossless ([speculative-decoding cost model, EVICT/EcoSpec/AcceptMoE](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.38981)).

The cheapest experiment in the whole graph comes before all of this. **Log unique experts per layer per verify block on the REAP-192 model.** No paper measures union growth after expert pruning. REAP should concentrate routing and raise overlap, but if DeepSeek-style routing holds near 3.9x k, only lossy budgets or a lower k will move chat.

## Prefix reuse beats prefill speed for agentic TTFT

For time-to-first-token, the step change is not a faster prefill but never redoing one. On real coding-agent traces the median session runs 43 turns with 142K input tokens, prefix-cache hit rates exceed **96%**, and 44% of sessions spawn subagents ([vLLM × AgentX](https://vllm.ai/blog/2026-09-08-vllm-agentx)). Copilot traces drop to about 55% at turn boundaries, because clients edit history ([MSR traces](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/ghcp_traces-6.pdf)). The server's single-chain "new prompt extends the old one" reuse misses both cases: a subagent return re-prefills the parent, and an edited history loses everything after the edit. A cold 32K prefill at 739 tok/s is about 43 s, while a session snapshot at about 1.7 KB/token is about 57 MB and restores from RAM or NVMe in well under a second. AttentionStore-style session caches report up to **87% lower TTFT** on multi-turn chat ([CachedAttention, ATC'24](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.19708v3)).

DeepSeek's V4.1 report describes the exact design. Persist the compressed global KV plus indexer K and **do not persist SWA KV**. On a hit, replay only the last 128 tokens (SWA Bounded Replay), which DeepSeek calls negligible in quality and which cuts the persistent footprint to about 1/8 of V4-Flash ([DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash report](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.19969)). SGLang's ShadowRadix gives the radix-tree mechanics: separate SWA/C4/C128 pools with two-counter locks and SWA-safe matching ([LMSYS](https://www.lmsys.org/blog/2026-04-25-deepseek-v4/)). vLLM shows that cache hits must land on 256-token compression-block boundaries ([vLLM DeepSeek V4](https://vllm.ai/blog/2026-04-24-deepseek-v4)). Two open bug reports are free test cases: 0% hits on prompts under 4,096 tokens, and MTP speculation shortening the hit length ([vllm-ascend #16270](https://github.com/vllm-project/vllm-ascend/issues/16270); [#9247](https://github.com/vllm-project/vllm-ascend/issues/9247)). On Thor's roughly 17 GiB of free RAM, an exact SWA snapshot is small enough to keep the hot tier bit-exact. Use bounded replay only for NVMe-tier entries. Non-prefix reuse (CacheBlend, EPIC) reports 2–8x TTFT for 1–7% quality loss on dense RoPE models ([EPIC](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.15332v3)). Nothing validates it on compressed, indexed, cross-layer-shared KV, so it stays off the graph.

The prefill and long-context literature mostly does not apply. Sparse-prefill methods are **slower than dense at 4K** (XAttention 0.79x, MInference and FlexPrefill about 0.12x, [FlashPrefill V2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.19758)), and V4.1 attention is already top-512 sparse over FP4 compressed KV. KV quantization and eviction are irrelevant: the 1M-token state is 1.66 GiB, and DeepSeek reports decode FLOPs rising only about 1/4 from 4K to 1M ([V4.1 report](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.19969)). The useful prefill work is local. Profile the indexer's O(N²/m) Full-layer scoring, which is the likely cause of the 739→580 tok/s fall from 7K to 8K (inferred from the architecture, not yet measured). Port vLLM's fused compressor/RoPE/insert kernels (1.4–3x per op) and a radix-select top-k (SGLang: >100 µs to about 15 µs at batch 1). SpecPrefill's 7.66x TTFT on Llama-405B is the only big multiplier left ([SpecPrefill](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.02789)). It breaks exact prefix caching and is unsafe on code and tool output, so restrict it to one-shot long documents if it is built at all. An independent 30,000-run study capped LLMLingua-style prompt compression at about 18% end to end ([arXiv 2604.02985](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.02985)).

Memory work centres on Engram. Its 196B FP8 parameters sit on NVMe, and its lookups are **deterministic from token IDs** ([V4.1 report](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.19969)). The measured 1.2 ms/token row store is plausibly IOPS-bound, about 48 rows/token into 4 KB O_DIRECT pages ([internal note](/home/patrickd/.claude/projects/-home-patrickd/memory/thor-base-model-shortlist.md)). It can be hidden in three ways: deduplicated, sorted io_uring batches over the whole prompt in prefill; fetching token t+1's rows the moment it is sampled; and fetching *drafted* tokens' rows speculatively, since verify needs all k+1. An LFU hot-row cache in free RAM is attractive, but nobody has published Engram row skew, so replay a local trace before sizing it. GPUDirect Storage does not support migratable unified memory, so keep pinned-buffer io_uring ([NVIDIA GDS docs](https://docs.nvidia.com/gpudirect-storage/index.html)).

## Vision is a build-out with one routing trap

Vision support in the CUDA server is engineering, not research, and llama.cpp's mmproj path already reads text in an image exactly on this model. The tower is 32 layers at width 1024 plus a 3x3-unfold aligner, 0.90 GiB in bf16 ([config.json](https://huggingface.co/deepseek-ai/DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash-1017/resolve/main/config.json)). LLM prefill of the image tokens dominates latency except at the 1,024-token maximum, where global attention over about 9,216 patches dominates the ViT. Build order: a bf16 ViT with fused online-softmax attention and in-kernel 2D RoPE on the tcgen05 path already proven here; the unfold fused into the aligner GEMM's operand gather; an encoder-output cache keyed by a hash of the resized pixel tensor; and image-hash-extended prefix-cache keys so follow-up turns never re-prefill the image, as vLLM does ([vLLM prefix caching](https://docs.vllm.ai/en/stable/design/prefix_caching.html)). Skip disaggregated encode: SGLang notes it can *raise* TTFT on image-light traffic ([LMSYS EPD](https://www.lmsys.org/blog/2026-01-12-epd)). W8A8 on the ViT is reported nearly lossless in NVILA ([arXiv 2412.04468](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2412.04468)), but it is a later, separately gated arm.

The trap is routing. Image rows select experts with the separate `bias_vl`, whose correlation with the text bias is only −0.05. Text-only expert ranking at 272 experts was worse than random for vision, and the REAP keep-set was ranked on mixed statistics ([local REPORT.md](/home/patrickd/deepseek-v4.1-flash-reap-thor/REPORT.md)). A router that applies the text bias to image rows degrades vision silently, so the golden test must include image prompts, and REAP-192 vision quality must be measured before any token reduction is added. On token reduction, uniform pooling or random selection matches or beats attention-score pruners for natural images, keeping **96.4% at 75% pruning** on LLaVA-1.5 ([Wen et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.11501v2)). OCR models break general pruners: RTPrune keeps 99.47% on documents only at about 16% pruning ([RTPrune](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.00392)). Because the aligner already merges 9 patches per token, default pruning off for documents and use a per-request resolution cap for photos. On Qwen3-VL-MoE, MoDES-style modality-specific top-k reduction on image rows gave 2.16x prefill and 1.26x decode ([MoDES](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.15690v1)). It is the one vision speed lever with MoE evidence, and it reuses the top-k machinery from the AR experiment.

The black-swan sweep found nothing that transfers at full strength. Jacobi Forcing gets about 4x on code and math, but only on dense 7B ([arXiv 2512.14681](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2512.14681)). ZAYA1's diffusion conversion took about 1.1T tokens on an 8B MoE ([Zyphra](https://www.zyphra.com/our-work/zaya1-8b-diffusion-preview)). Both are multi-token forwards that pay the same expert-union price, and the training bill is out of reach. Most 2–5x "MoE speedup" headlines are measured against expert *offloading*, which Thor does not do. The one place that literature applies is the Engram NVMe tier. Ternary post-training quantization costs about 10 points at 4B and collapses math and code at MoE scale under generic calibration ([arXiv 2608.01078](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.01078)). The real "black swan" here is mundane: the router-renormalization fix. If it holds on REAP-192 under generative evals, it is the only single change that lifts both AR and spec decode at once.

## Conclusion

This research changes the question the server's development graph should ask. "How do we accept more draft tokens?" was the right question for dense targets. For a fine-grained DeepSeek MoE at batch 1 on unified memory, the right one is **"how many distinct expert bytes does each committed token cost?"** That reframing explains past results: the 0.98x chat result, the 1.27x GLM-5.3 ceiling and the negative drafter fine-tune all follow from it. It also makes the owner's adaptive spec idea more valuable than it first looks. As a per-request switch it is a 2% insurance policy. As a per-round cost-aware controller it becomes the integration point where top-k reduction, verifier budgets and suffix drafts all turn into speed, because each one lowers the break-even line the controller optimizes against.

The main open risks are empirical and cheap to resolve. Nobody has measured verify-block expert unions on an expert-pruned model, top-k halving on a DeepSeek-family or REAP model, or Engram row skew. The first week of the graph should be instrumentation, not kernels. Those three measurements decide which half of the table carries the next 1.5x, and whether a lossy verify mode is needed at all. For TTFT, the session prefix cache is the lowest-risk large win in the set. It is the one item where DeepSeek has already published the design for this exact architecture.
